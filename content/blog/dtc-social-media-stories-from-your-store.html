In Adobe Express's survey of more than 400 business owners, 7 in 10 said they felt burnt out by content creation and 68% had taken a break from posting because of it. Only 18% posted every day; 44% posted once a week. Constant Contact found that 47% of small business owners run their company's social media themselves. If you sell on Shopify or Amazon, that's probably you, fitting it in between orders and stock.

● In short
  • Stop starting from a blank page. Every restock, review, launch and best seller is a story.
  • Make one vertical video or frame, then cut it to each channel's shape: 9:16, 4:5 and 2:3 cover almost everything.
  • Put a tracked link on every story so you can see which ones sold, and spend ad money only on those.

Your store is the content calendar

The posts that work for small brands are rarely clever. They're news from the store, told plainly. These happen every week whether you plan them or not:

Check stock before you post. Promoting a product you're about to run out of wastes the attention you just earned; see days of cover.

Shoot or build everything vertical, then crop. One 9:16 story becomes a Reel, a TikTok and a YouTube Short; a 4:5 crop goes in the feed; a 2:3 version goes to Pinterest.

Posting to every channel automatically

You don't have to open five apps. The platforms let approved tools post for you, with a few limits worth knowing:

  • Instagram lets tools publish Stories, Reels and feed posts, but Stories only for Business accounts, not Creator accounts. Check which one you have in the app's account settings.
  • Facebook Pages can publish photo and video stories and Reels the same way.
  • TikTok keeps posts from a tool private until TikTok has audited that tool, so ask any scheduler whether it's audited.
  • YouTube treats a short vertical upload as a Short; there's no separate upload.
  • Pinterest accepts video pins from tools, though uploads are less reliable than images.

Meta also lets you label AI-generated images and video when you post. If a scene in your story was generated rather than filmed, label it.

Track which stories sold

Likes don't pay for stock. Give every story its own link (a UTM tag or a short code) and check Shopify a day later: sessions, orders and revenue from that story. After a few weeks you'll know which kinds of story sell for your brand, and the calendar writes itself.

When an organic story clearly beats your usual results, that's the one to put money behind. Meta lets you boost an existing post or story as an ad, so you're spending on something already proven rather than guessing. Then check the ad against real orders, not Meta's own numbers; see ROAS against real sales.

Rules that apply to stories too

  • Customer posts: ask before reposting and credit the person. Don't edit what they said.
  • Reviews: don't show only the good ones if you're presenting them as typical, and say so if a review was incentivised. The FTC's review rule carries real penalties; see our compliance calendar.
  • Comparisons: “lasts twice as long” needs a test behind it.
  • Creators: paid or gifted posts need a clear disclosure, such as the platform's paid-partnership label.
