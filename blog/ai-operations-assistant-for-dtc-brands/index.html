47 ops jobs you can hand to an AI assistant, and the ones you shouldn't

Most of what an e-commerce operations hire does is watching, reconciling, chasing and filing. That is exactly the work an always-on assistant is good at. Here is the full list, sorted by where the money is.

By the Femp team · November 15, 2026 · 5 min read

If you run a Shopify brand, and probably an Amazon store next to it, your day is split across eight to twelve tools: Shopify admin, Seller Central, Meta Ads Manager, Google Ads, Klaviyo, your 3PL portal, your inbox, your bank and a spreadsheet that holds it all together. Each one has a dashboard. None of them tells you what you should do this morning.

That gap is why brands hire their first operations person. We read about forty job postings for e-commerce coordinators, DTC operations managers, Amazon account managers and offshore virtual assistants to see what that person actually does. Roughly two-thirds of the listed duties are monitoring, reconciling, chasing and filing. The rest is creative work, strategy and negotiation.

The first group is what an AI operations assistant can take over. This guide lists those jobs in five groups, ordered by where the money is.

● In short
  • An ops assistant earns its keep on money in (ads, carts, chargebacks), money leaking (fees, overcharges, deductions) and deadlines (stock-outs, disputes, filings).
  • It should reach you where you already are (WhatsApp or text), with the fix ready, and act only after you say yes.
  • Creative, brand and campaign strategy stay with people. The assistant hands those off cleanly.

What a day looks like

A morning message with yesterday's numbers and the three or four decisions that need you. Interruptions during the day only when money or a deadline is at stake. A weekly review on Friday. Each time you reply with a number or a "yes", and the assistant does the work, logs it and remembers your preference.

1. Growth: money on the table

These are the jobs an owner pays for because they show up in revenue.

  • Ad budget checks. Flag ad sets below your ROAS floor for several days, compared with what actually sold in the store rather than what the ad platform claims. How to check ROAS against real sales.
  • Chargebacks. Catch every dispute, gather tracking, delivery proof and customer messages, and draft the response before the deadline. Answering every dispute.
  • Abandoned-cart recovery. Most carts are abandoned (Baymard's long-running average is about 70%). The flows exist; the job is noticing when one breaks and picking high-value carts, such as repeat buyers, for a personal offer.
  • Win-back and churn. Spot lapsed repeat buyers and subscribers who skipped cycles, and draft the win-back.
  • Restock waitlists. When a sold-out item is back, tell the people who asked.
  • Carrier refunds, 3PL overcharges and retailer deductions. Money you already earned and quietly lose. Where it hides.

2. Operations: the day keeps running

  • Inventory and reorders. Days of cover per item against supplier lead time, a reorder suggestion, and a nudge when the ETA slips. Days of cover, explained.
  • Amazon account health. Suppressed listings, policy warnings, IP complaints and Buy Box loss, with a fix drafted inside Amazon's 72-hour Account Health Assurance window.
  • Shipping and 3PL exceptions. Orders stuck for two days, mis-picks and backorders, with the escalation drafted and customers told before they ask.
  • Refund approvals. Refunds over your limit and VIP complaints come to you with context; everything else stays in the helpdesk.
  • Supplier follow-ups. Chase ETAs, samples and quotes, and recalculate landed cost when duties change. With the US $800 de minimis exemption gone since 2025, landed cost moves more than it used to.
  • Listing and site audits. Broken links, stale promos, policy words that get listings suppressed.

3. Analytics: numbers you see without asking

  • Morning brief. Sales by channel, ad spend and return on it, cash and upcoming payouts, plus the decisions due today.
  • Margin by product and channel. Contribution margin after ads, shipping, fulfillment, fees and returns, so you know which products lose money.
  • Customer metrics. New versus repeat buyers, time to second order, cohort retention, with the change explained in a sentence.
  • Weekly review. Revenue, margin, average order value, repeat rate, returns by product, and what to do next week.

4. Housekeeping

  • App audit. Paid apps you haven't opened in 60 days.
  • Inbox triage. Separate chargebacks, supplier delays, account warnings and reviews from the noise, and draft the replies.
  • Renewals. Domain, trademark, insurance, carrier and 3PL contracts, with time to renegotiate.
  • SOPs. Write down how a task was done so a person can take it over.

5. Accounting and regulatory

Every item here has a date, a penalty or a lawsuit behind it, and almost none of it lives in the tools you open daily: sales tax nexus, quarterly estimates, 1099s, state annual reports, SMS consent, packaging EPR, accessibility. The assistant is not your accountant or lawyer. Its job is to know your calendar, chase the documents they need, and say plainly when a rule changed. The DTC compliance calendar.

What should stay human

Creative, brand voice, campaign strategy, supplier negotiation and lifecycle experiments. An assistant can monitor a campaign and draft a brief, but deciding what your brand says is your job, or your agency's. A good assistant knows the difference and hands off cleanly.
